South Asia Climate Trend: 12 Data-Backed Findings From 30 Years of City-Level Records

Published August 17, 2026 · Cross-Regional Analysis · Updated with each new city report

Most overviews of the "South Asia climate trend" work from region-wide averages — a single warming figure for the whole subcontinent, often drawn from century-scale or decade-old estimates. That approach hides more than it reveals. A region spanning coastal megacities, Himalayan valleys, and inland plains doesn't warm — or get wetter, or drier — as one unit.

This report takes a different approach: it's built entirely from our own city-by-city ERA5/ERA5-Land reanalysis data, covering 1995–2025 across eight South Asian cities — Karachi, Delhi, Dhaka, Colombo, Kathmandu, Lahore, Islamabad, and Mumbai. Below are twelve findings that emerge from comparing them directly.

A note on data sources: widely cited regional figures — such as the often-referenced ~0.75°C per century warming estimate for South Asia from mid-2010s cost-of-adaptation studies — are useful for broad policy framing but are now over a decade old and necessarily average across enormous geographic and climatic diversity. The findings below use more recent (1995–2025), higher-resolution, city-specific reanalysis data, and are best read as a complement to — not a replacement for — that regional literature.

12 Findings From the Data

Finding 1

South Asia is warming, but nowhere near uniformly

Annual warming rates across the 8 cities in this dataset range from an essentially flat, slightly negative trend in Kathmandu (~−0.04°C/decade) to a steep +0.77°C/decade in Islamabad — nearly a twenty-fold spread within one region.

Finding 2

Islamabad is the fastest-warming city studied, by a wide margin

At roughly +0.77°C per decade, Islamabad warms about six times faster than Karachi or Lahore (~0.13°C/decade each) and more than double Dhaka's already-fast 0.30°C/decade — likely a combination of regional warming and the city's rapid, recent urbanization.

Finding 3

Winter is the region's outlier season — usually stable, except in one city

In every city studied except Islamabad, winter is the slowest-warming or most stable season. In Islamabad alone, winter warms fastest of all four seasons (~+1.05°C/decade) — a genuinely unusual regional pattern worth watching for signs it's spreading.

Finding 4

Where winter isn't the story, monsoon and post-monsoon usually are

Dhaka, Karachi, Colombo, Kathmandu, and Lahore all show their fastest warming during the monsoon; Delhi's fastest season is post-monsoon. Mumbai is the exception among exceptions, warming fastest in the pre-monsoon hot season.

Finding 5

Rainfall trends split the region roughly in half

Mumbai (~+300mm/decade), Colombo (~+170mm/decade), Karachi (~+88.7mm/decade), Islamabad (~+60mm/decade), and Kathmandu (~+50mm/decade) are all getting measurably wetter. Dhaka stands apart, showing a declining precipitation trend (~−140mm/decade) despite being one of the fastest-warming cities in the dataset.

Finding 6

Mumbai's rainfall trend is the steepest recorded anywhere in this series

At roughly +300mm per decade, Mumbai's precipitation increase is more than three times Karachi's — the next-highest coastal city — pointing to a directly intensifying Arabian Sea monsoon rather than a region-wide rainfall pattern.

Finding 7

Extreme heat above 40°C is concentrated inland, not coastal

Delhi (20–50+ days/year) and Lahore (20–46 days/year) see extreme heat routinely. Coastal or moderated cities — Mumbai, Colombo, Karachi, Dhaka — see it rarely or almost never, even where annual mean warming is comparable.

Finding 8

Heat and rainfall trends don't move together

Mumbai is getting hotter and wetter. Dhaka is getting hotter and drier. There is no single "South Asia climate trend" for the heat-water relationship — each city's combination of warming and precipitation change is its own story.

Finding 9

Kathmandu is the dataset's genuine outlier — a flat-to-cooling annual trend

Alone among the 8 cities, Kathmandu shows an annual trend near zero (~−0.04°C/decade), even as its monsoon season still warms. Elevation, valley topography, and local land-use effects are plausible contributors that a regional average would completely obscure.

Finding 10

Urbanization is a real confound in the fastest-warming cities

Islamabad's outsized warming rate, and to a lesser extent trends in other rapidly built-up cities, likely reflect a mix of genuine regional climate change and local urban heat island effects from decades of construction — a limitation that 9–25km reanalysis grids can only partially resolve.

Finding 11

Recent years show sharper jumps than smooth long-term trends suggest

Several cities in this dataset — Mumbai's winter is the clearest example, rising roughly 2.2°C between 2022 and 2025 alone — show multi-year step-changes that a 30-year linear trend line understates. The direction of change and its recent acceleration are often more informative than the average slope alone.

Finding 12

"South Asia climate trend" is really eight distinct, data-specific stories

The most defensible summary of this dataset isn't a single number — it's that every city combines its own warming rate, seasonal driver, and rainfall direction differently. Regional averages are useful for policy framing, but city-level data is what actually explains what residents of Karachi, Mumbai, or Kathmandu are experiencing.

Data Source & Methodology

All figures in this report are derived from the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, built on ERA5 and ERA5-Land reanalysis data produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) through the Copernicus Climate Change Service, covering January 1995 through 2025. Trend rates are estimated by reading the linear regression trend line plotted on each city's chart across the full study period.

Seasonal boundaries follow the standard South Asian meteorological convention: Winter (December–February), Pre-Monsoon/Hot (March–May), Monsoon (June–September), and Post-Monsoon (October–November). This report will be updated as additional cities are added to the series.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the climate trend in South Asia?

Based on 30 years of ERA5 reanalysis data (1995–2025) across 8 South Asian cities, the region is warming overall, but unevenly — city-level annual warming rates range from roughly −0.04°C per decade in Kathmandu to +0.77°C per decade in Islamabad. Rainfall trends diverge even more sharply, with some cities getting wetter (Mumbai, Karachi) and others drier (Dhaka).

Is South Asia getting warmer?

Yes, most cities studied show a warming trend, though the pace varies widely by location — from a nearly flat trend in Kathmandu to a steep 0.77°C per decade in Islamabad, the fastest-warming city in this dataset.

Which South Asian city is warming fastest?

Islamabad, Pakistan shows the fastest annual warming rate among the 8 cities analyzed, at approximately +0.77°C per decade — roughly six times the rate seen in Karachi or Lahore.

Is South Asia's monsoon getting stronger?

It depends on the city. Mumbai shows the steepest monsoon-season rainfall increase in this dataset (roughly +300mm per decade annually), while Dhaka shows declining precipitation despite rising temperatures — indicating no single regional monsoon trend.

How much has South Asia warmed in the last 30 years?

This varies enormously by city in the 1995–2025 ERA5 dataset: from an essentially flat trend in Kathmandu to a rise of roughly 2.3°C over 30 years in Islamabad, illustrating why city-level data tells a more accurate story than a single regional average.
