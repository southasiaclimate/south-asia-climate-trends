Islamabad Annual Rainfall: 30 Years of Trend Data (1996–2025)

Published September 7, 2026 · Islamabad, Pakistan
Quick answer: Islamabad's annual rainfall typically falls between 800mm and 1,300mm, with a 30-year ERA5 trend line rising from around 900mm in the mid-1990s to roughly 1,080mm by 2025 — about +60mm per decade. But that headline number hides a genuine split: the increase is driven almost entirely by the monsoon season, while winter — Islamabad's fastest-warming season by a wide margin — is actually seeing rainfall decline slightly.

Islamabad already holds the distinction of the fastest overall annual warming rate of any lowland city in this series (~+0.77°C/decade), and its winter warming (~+1.23°C/decade) is the fastest of any season measured anywhere in this dataset. Given that, it would be reasonable to assume winter rainfall is rising too. It isn't. This report covers Islamabad's full rainfall picture — the annual trend, the season driving it, and the one season moving in the opposite direction from what the temperature data alone might suggest — as a companion to our full Islamabad climate trend report.

How Much Does It Rain in Islamabad Each Year?

Annual totals are among the highest of any city in this series, reflecting Islamabad's position at the foothills of the Himalaya where both monsoon and western disturbance systems contribute rainfall. Totals range from roughly 615mm in the driest years to a record 1,440mm.

Key Insight

Winter warms fastest — but its rainfall is declining

This is the most notable finding in Islamabad's data. Winter (December–February) shows the fastest-warming trend of any season in this entire series (~+1.23°C/decade, according to our full climate trend report), yet winter precipitation is trending slightly downward (~−10mm/decade) over the same 30 years. These are two different atmospheric signals: winter temperature in this region responds strongly to broader regional and global warming, while winter precipitation depends heavily on the frequency and strength of western disturbances — a separate weather system that isn't necessarily tracking the same trend. The practical takeaway: a warmer winter in Islamabad does not necessarily mean a wetter one.

The monsoon tells the opposite story — it's both a large contributor to Islamabad's total rainfall and the season with the clearest rising trend, punctuated by 2025's record monsoon total of roughly 865mm, alone nearly matching the entire annual rainfall of a dry year like 2000 or 2009.

A note on extreme heat: Islamabad's extreme-heat-day count (≥40°C) remains low and noisy across the record — mostly 0 to 11 days a year, with a sharp single-year spike to roughly 20 days in 2012. This is consistent with Islamabad's cooler, higher-elevation setting relative to Lahore or Delhi, and doesn't show a clear multi-decade trend either way.

Data Source & Methodology

All figures in this report are derived from the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, built on ERA5 and ERA5-Land reanalysis data produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) through the Copernicus Climate Change Service, covering January 1996 through 2025. Trend rates are estimated by reading the linear regression trend line plotted on each chart across the full study period. Seasonal boundaries follow the standard South Asian meteorological convention: Winter (December–February), Pre-Monsoon/Hot (March–May), Monsoon (June–September), and Post-Monsoon (October–November). Annual warming-rate figures referenced here are drawn from our full Islamabad climate trend report.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much rain does Islamabad get per year?

Based on 1996–2025 ERA5 reanalysis data, Islamabad's annual rainfall typically falls between 800mm and 1,300mm, with a 30-year trend line rising from around 900mm to roughly 1,080mm.

Is Islamabad's rainfall increasing?

Yes, at the annual level — approximately +60mm per decade — but this is driven almost entirely by the monsoon season (+57mm/decade). Winter rainfall, by contrast, is trending slightly downward even as winter is Islamabad's fastest-warming season.

Why is Islamabad's winter rainfall declining while winter temperature rises so fast?

These are two different atmospheric signals that don't have to move together. Winter temperature in Islamabad is rising faster than any other season in this entire series (~+1.23°C/decade), largely independent of western disturbance activity, which governs winter precipitation and shows a modest declining trend (~−10mm/decade) over the same period.

What is the wettest year on record for Islamabad?

2020 is the wettest year in the 1996–2025 ERA5 record, at roughly 1,440mm — well above any other year in the dataset.

Which season drives Islamabad's rising rainfall trend?

The monsoon (June–September), which shows the steepest seasonal precipitation increase (~+57mm/decade) and includes 2025's record monsoon total of roughly 865mm — nearly matching the entire annual rainfall of a dry year like 2000 or 2009.
