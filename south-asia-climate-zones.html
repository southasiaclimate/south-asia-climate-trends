South Asia's Climate Zones: What Type of Climate Does Each City Have?

Published September 13, 2026 · Regional Reference

South Asia packs an unusually wide range of climate types into one region — from true desert to tropical rainforest to subtropical highland, often within a few hundred kilometers of each other. This report uses the Köppen-Geiger climate classification, the world's most widely used system for categorizing climate types based on temperature and rainfall patterns, to place each city in this series into its correct zone — including resolving a couple of genuinely disputed cases.

An important distinction: Köppen classifications are based on long-term historical averages — they describe a city's baseline climate type, not how it's changing. That's exactly the gap our own 30-year ERA5 trend data fills: this page tells you what zone each city sits in; our full city reports show you how each one is actually moving within — or toward the edge of — that zone.

City by City

BWh

Karachi — Hot Desert

Low annual rainfall (typically under 250mm in most years) paired with consistently high temperatures year-round, moderated somewhat by Karachi's coastal position on the Arabian Sea. See our full Karachi climate trend report and rainfall deep-dive — where an extreme outlier year like 2022 (~830mm) shows just how dramatically a desert climate can depart from its own norm.

BSh

Lahore — Hot Semi-Arid (Steppe)

More rainfall than a true desert climate but still dry overall, with hot summers and a pronounced dry season. See our Lahore rainfall & heat report for how this plays out in practice — including why the city runs so hot even with a nearly flat 30-year rainfall trend.

Cwa / BSh

Delhi — Humid Subtropical (Borderline)

Delhi is a genuinely disputed case. Most modern academic sources classify it Cwa (humid subtropical, monsoon-influenced, dry winter), defined by having at least one month averaging above 22°C and enough summer rainfall to avoid the "B" (dry) group. But several climate-normal databases classify it BSh (hot semi-arid) instead, given Delhi's relatively modest annual rainfall (~700–800mm) sits close to the threshold between the two categories. Both classifications are commonly cited — Delhi sits right on the line. See our Delhi climate trend report, rainfall report, and extreme heat report.

Cwa

Islamabad — Humid Subtropical

Similar classification to Delhi, but with meaningfully higher rainfall thanks to its position at the Himalayan foothills, catching both monsoon and western disturbance systems. See our Islamabad climate trend report and our explainer on its unusual winter warming anomaly.

Aw

Dhaka — Tropical Savanna

Warm year-round with a pronounced wet monsoon season and a genuine dry season — the classic tropical savanna pattern. See our full Dhaka climate trend report, and our Dhaka vs. Karachi comparison for how this tropical climate's trends actually diverge from Karachi's desert one.

Am

Mumbai — Tropical Monsoon

Warm temperatures year-round with a short, genuine dry season and an intense wet monsoon delivering the large majority of annual rainfall — textbook Am. See our full Mumbai climate trend report and Mumbai vs. Karachi comparison.

Af

Colombo — Tropical Rainforest (Not "Tropical Monsoon")

This is worth clarifying directly: Colombo is classified Af (tropical rainforest), not Am (tropical monsoon), by most current sources. The distinction comes down to one technical criterion — Af requires every single month to receive at least 60mm of rain, with no genuine dry season, while Am requires at least one markedly drier month. Colombo meets the Af threshold: even its driest month typically clears 60mm. Some informal or older sources describe Colombo loosely as "tropical monsoon" because its rainfall pattern is monsoon-influenced in timing — but under the strict Köppen system, Af is the correct classification. See our full Colombo climate trend report and rainfall deep-dive, where Colombo is confirmed as the wettest city in this entire series on a typical-year basis.

Cwb

Kathmandu — Subtropical Highland

Kathmandu's ~1,400m elevation cools it well below what its latitude alone would suggest, producing a mild, temperate highland climate rather than the subtropical heat found in nearby lowland cities. Fittingly, Kathmandu is also the only location in this series without a clear warming trend — see our explainer on why Kathmandu isn't warming like its neighbors.

Cwb-adjacent

Rasuwagadhi — Himalayan Highland

At roughly 1,880m — meaningfully higher than Kathmandu — Rasuwagadhi's exact Köppen subtype is more elevation-sensitive and less standard to pin down precisely than the lowland cities in this series; it sits somewhere in the cool highland range bordering true mountain/subarctic classifications. What the data shows clearly, regardless of exact classification: the fastest warming rate and steepest rainfall decline of any location in this entire series.

Why Classification Alone Isn't the Full Story

A Köppen zone tells you what a city's climate has typically looked like, averaged over many decades — it doesn't tell you whether that climate is shifting, or how fast. Karachi is still classified as a hot desert climate, for instance, but our own data shows its rainfall trend rising at +88.7mm per decade, disproportionately driven by extreme years like 2022. Delhi's borderline Cwa/BSh classification is itself a hint that small shifts in rainfall could nudge its classification in either direction over time. This is exactly the kind of change a static climate-zone map won't show you, but 30 years of year-by-year reanalysis data will.

Data Source & Methodology

Köppen-Geiger classifications referenced in this report follow the standard system first published by Wladimir Köppen and widely used in climate science and geography. Classification boundaries are based on long-term (multi-decade) climate normals rather than this site's own 30-year ERA5 dataset, though the two are consistent with each other. Trend figures linked throughout are drawn from our own analysis using the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, built on ERA5 and ERA5-Land reanalysis data from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) via the Copernicus Climate Change Service, covering 1995/96 through 2025.

Frequently Asked Questions

What climate zone is Delhi in?

Delhi sits near the boundary of two Köppen classifications: most modern sources classify it as Cwa (humid subtropical, monsoon-influenced, dry winter), while some climate-normal databases classify it as BSh (hot semi-arid) given its relatively low annual rainfall (~700–800mm) for a "C" group climate. Both are commonly cited; Delhi is genuinely borderline between the two categories.

Is Colombo a tropical monsoon or tropical rainforest climate?

Tropical rainforest (Af), by most current classifications. Colombo receives at least 60mm of rain in every single month of the year with no genuine dry season, which is the defining criterion for Af rather than Am (tropical monsoon, which requires at least one markedly drier month). Some older or informal sources describe Colombo as "tropical monsoon" loosely, referring to its monsoon-influenced rainfall pattern rather than the strict Köppen Am category.

What is the climate of South Asia?

South Asia spans an unusually wide range of Köppen climate zones for one region — from Karachi's hot desert (BWh) and Lahore's hot semi-arid (BSh) climate, through Delhi and Islamabad's humid subtropical (Cwa) zones, to Dhaka's tropical savanna (Aw), Mumbai's tropical monsoon (Am), Colombo's tropical rainforest (Af), and Kathmandu's subtropical highland (Cwb) climate shaped by elevation.

What climate zone is Karachi in?

Karachi has a hot desert climate (Köppen BWh) — low annual rainfall (typically under 250mm in most years) and consistently high temperatures, moderated somewhat by its coastal position on the Arabian Sea.

Do Köppen climate zones account for climate change?

No — Köppen classifications are based on multi-decade historical averages and represent a static snapshot, not a trend. A city's classification can shift over time as its climate changes, but the standard Köppen maps don't show that shift directly. That's the gap this site's own 30-year ERA5 trend data is built to fill — showing not just what climate zone a city is in, but how its temperature and rainfall are actually moving within that zone.
