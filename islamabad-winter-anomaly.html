Islamabad's Winter Warming Anomaly, Explained

Published September 11, 2026 · Islamabad, Pakistan
Quick answer: Islamabad's winter (December–February) is warming at approximately +1.05°C per decade — the fastest seasonal trend of any lowland city in this 9-location series, and a complete reversal of the pattern found everywhere else. In every other South Asian city studied here, winter is the slowest-changing or even cooling season. In Islamabad, it's the fastest by a wide margin. The likely explanation is genuinely two things at once: real regional climate change, and rapid local urbanization — and the data alone can't fully separate them.

This finding first surfaced in our full Islamabad climate trend report, where it stood out immediately: Islamabad's annual warming rate (+0.77°C/decade) is already the steepest of any lowland city in this series, roughly six times Karachi's or Lahore's. But break it down by season, and winter alone outpaces even that dramatic annual figure. This report goes deeper into that single finding — what the data shows, why it's unusual, and what plausibly explains it.

The Numbers

Winter mean temperature climbs from a trend-line baseline near 9.7°C in the mid-1990s to roughly 12.9°C by 2025. The coldest winter on record, 1995, reached just 8.9°C; the warmest, 2025, reached 13.8°C — nearly a 5°C spread between the two ends of a single 30-year record for the same season.

Key Figure: Winter Warming Rate

Approximately +1.05°C per decade

For context: even Islamabad's slowest-warming season (monsoon, at +0.57°C/decade) would be the fastest seasonal warming rate recorded anywhere else in this series except Dhaka's monsoon. Winter, at nearly double that slowest-season rate, is in a category of its own.

Why This Is Unusual

Across every other city in this series, warming is concentrated in the monsoon or post-monsoon season — winter is consistently the most stable, or in Kathmandu's case, actually cooling.

Islamabad and Rasuwagadhi are the only two locations in this entire series where winter leads. That's not a coincidence worth ignoring — both sit at meaningfully higher elevation than the lowland cities studied elsewhere, pointing toward elevation and topography as a real factor in why winter behaves so differently in these two places.

Two Explanations, Both Likely True

Our full Islamabad report is explicit about this, and it's worth restating clearly: the scale of this number demands caution, not alarm alone.

  • Genuine regional climate signal. Winter warming this pronounced, paired with Rasuwagadhi showing an even steeper winter trend at much higher elevation, suggests a real atmospheric pattern affecting this part of the Himalayan foothill region specifically — not just Islamabad in isolation.
  • Rapid urbanization. Islamabad is a relatively young, planned city, founded in the 1960s, that has seen substantial construction and land-use change across this exact 30-year study window. Urban heat island effects — where expanding built infrastructure raises local temperatures independent of broader climate trends — are a well-documented phenomenon, and they tend to show up most clearly in winter minimum temperatures specifically, which is consistent with what this data shows.

Reanalysis data at roughly 9–25km grid resolution can only partially separate these two effects from each other. The honest reading of this dataset is that both are very likely contributing simultaneously, rather than one fully explaining the other.

The Rainfall Disconnect

As covered in our dedicated Islamabad annual rainfall report, winter's dramatic temperature rise has no matching rainfall trend — if anything, the opposite. Winter precipitation shows a slight decline (~−8mm/decade) over the same 30 years.

Key Insight

A warmer Islamabad winter is not a wetter one

Winter temperature responds strongly to whatever combination of regional warming and urbanization is driving this trend; winter precipitation depends on a separate factor — the frequency and strength of western disturbance weather systems — that isn't tracking the same direction. The two aren't contradictory; they're simply different atmospheric drivers moving independently.

What This Means

  • This deserves dedicated local research, not just a data flag. Whether the driver is regional atmospheric patterns, elevation effects from the nearby Margalla Hills, urbanization concentrated in ways that show up more in winter data, or some combination, is a question this dataset can identify but not fully answer on its own.
  • Winter can no longer be treated as Islamabad's "safe," stable season. In most of the cities in this series, winter is the season with the least year-to-year climate risk. In Islamabad, it's the opposite — the season changing fastest of all.
  • The pattern isn't unique to Islamabad — Rasuwagadhi's even steeper winter trend suggests this may be a genuine feature of this specific sub-region (Islamabad-adjacent foothills and the high Himalaya beyond it), worth watching for in future data from similar elevation bands.

Data Source & Methodology

All figures in this report are drawn from our full Islamabad climate trend report, built on the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API using ERA5 and ERA5-Land reanalysis data from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) via the Copernicus Climate Change Service, covering January 1995 through 2025. Trend rates are estimated by reading the linear regression trend line plotted on each chart. Seasonal boundaries follow the standard South Asian meteorological convention: Winter (December–February), Pre-Monsoon/Hot (March–May), Monsoon (June–September), and Post-Monsoon (October–November). Given the unusually steep magnitude of these figures relative to every other city in this series, they should be read as a real, measured pattern likely reflecting a combination of regional climate warming and rapid local urbanization — not attributed to either cause alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast is Islamabad's winter warming?

Approximately +1.05°C per decade — the fastest-warming season of any lowland city in this 9-location series, rising from a trend-line baseline near 9.7°C in the mid-1990s to roughly 12.9°C by 2025.

Why is Islamabad's winter warming so much faster than other cities' winters?

The exact cause isn't settled by trend data alone, but two factors are likely both contributing: genuine regional climate warming, and rapid urbanization — Islamabad is a relatively young, planned city (founded in the 1960s) that has seen significant construction and land-use change over the same 30-year study period, and urban heat island effects can disproportionately affect winter minimum temperatures.

Is Islamabad's winter warming just an urban heat island effect?

Not purely — the honest answer is that reanalysis data at 9–25km resolution can only partially separate local urban effects from genuine regional climate signals. The pattern is real and measured, but very likely reflects a combination of both factors rather than either one alone.

Does Islamabad's winter rainfall show the same trend as its temperature?

No — this is one of the most notable findings in the data. While winter temperature is rising faster than any other season, winter rainfall is trending slightly downward (approximately −8mm per decade) over the same period. A warmer winter in Islamabad does not mean a wetter one.

Is Islamabad the only location with winter as its fastest-warming season?

No — Rasuwagadhi, a high-altitude Himalayan border location in this series, also shows winter as its fastest-warming season, at an even steeper rate (~+1.67°C/decade). Both locations sit at meaningfully higher elevation than the lowland cities in this series, suggesting elevation may be linked to this unusual seasonal pattern.
