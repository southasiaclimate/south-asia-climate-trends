Why Isn't Kathmandu Warming Like Its Neighbors?

Published September 10, 2026 · Kathmandu, Nepal
Quick answer: Kathmandu's annual mean temperature trend is essentially flat over 1995–2025 (~−0.04°C per decade) — the only location in this entire 9-city series without a clear warming signal. But "not warming" oversimplifies it: two seasons (winter and pre-monsoon) are genuinely cooling, while the monsoon is warming at a rate that matches its lowland neighbors almost exactly. Kathmandu isn't immune to regional climate change — the effect is concentrated in one season instead of spread evenly across the year.

Every other city in this series — Karachi, Lahore, Delhi, Dhaka, Islamabad, Colombo, Mumbai, and the Himalayan border crossing at Rasuwagadhi — shows a clear positive annual warming trend, ranging from a modest +0.13°C/decade up to a striking +0.90°C/decade. Kathmandu is the sole exception. This report digs into why, using the same 30-year ERA5/ERA5-Land reanalysis methodology applied throughout this site, drawing on our full Kathmandu climate trend report.

The Core Finding: Flat Annually, Divergent Seasonally

Kathmandu's annual record is volatile but directionless — the coldest year (1997, 17.15°C) and warmest year (2010, 19.0°C) sit less than 2°C apart, with no clear multi-decade climb connecting them. A notably cool stretch from 2017–2021 gives way to a partial recovery in the final two years, but the overall 30-year trend line stays essentially flat.

Compare this to Karachi (+0.13°C/decade) or Dhaka (+0.30°C/decade) — Kathmandu shows no meaningful annual warming signal, and if anything, a very slight cooling lean.

Two seasons — covering roughly half the year — are genuinely cooling, at nearly identical rates. The other two are flat to warming, with monsoon carrying essentially all of Kathmandu's positive warming signal. Its monsoon warming rate (+0.20°C/decade) "matches Karachi's monsoon warming almost exactly," according to our full climate trend report — strong evidence that Kathmandu isn't disconnected from the region's broader monsoon-season warming pattern. The anomaly is specifically a dry-season, non-monsoon story.

A concerning pairing: winter cooling arrives alongside a real decline in winter rainfall (from a trend-line baseline near 140mm in the mid-1990s down to roughly 50mm by 2025). Winter precipitation and snowpack matter for regional river flow into the dry season — a colder and drier winter trend is worth monitoring for water security implications, independent of whether the broader story is "warming" or not.

What Might Explain This?

Establishing a definitive physical cause requires research beyond what a reanalysis trend chart alone can support, but several plausible, non-exclusive factors are worth naming:

  • Valley topography and cold-air pooling. Kathmandu sits in a bowl-shaped mountain valley — a setting well known for trapping cold, dense air near the surface during calm winter and dry-season conditions (temperature inversions), a local effect that open lowland cities like Delhi or Karachi simply don't experience.
  • Reanalysis resolution limits in complex terrain. ERA5's ~9–25km grid resolution can struggle to fully resolve valley-specific microclimate effects — a caveat that matters more here than in the flatter cities studied elsewhere in this series, and one we flag explicitly in Kathmandu's full report.
  • A seasonally specific circulation shift, not a Kathmandu-specific immunity. Since monsoon-season warming tracks regional patterns closely, whatever is suppressing winter and pre-monsoon warming appears to act on dry-season atmospheric dynamics specifically — possibly related to the same western disturbance systems that also complicate winter precipitation trends in other Himalaya-adjacent locations in this series.

The Rasuwagadhi Contrast: Same Country, Opposite Story

The starkest comparison in this entire series isn't between Kathmandu and a distant city — it's with Rasuwagadhi, a Himalayan border crossing roughly 1,300m higher in elevation, in the same country.

This underscores a theme running through this entire series: elevation and specific topographic exposure shape climate trends at least as much as country or even regional proximity — a broad "South Asia is warming" narrative genuinely doesn't apply uniformly, even within a single country's borders.

Data Source & Methodology

All figures in this report are drawn from our full Kathmandu climate trend report, built on the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API using ERA5 and ERA5-Land reanalysis data from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) via the Copernicus Climate Change Service, covering January 1995 through 2025. Trend rates are estimated by reading the linear regression trend line plotted on each chart. Seasonal boundaries follow the standard South Asian meteorological convention: Winter (December–February), Pre-Monsoon/Hot (March–May), Monsoon (June–September), and Post-Monsoon (October–November). Kathmandu's complex mountain-valley terrain means ERA5's ~9–25km grid resolution carries more uncertainty here than for the flatter lowland cities in this series.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Kathmandu warming due to climate change?

Kathmandu's annual mean temperature trend is essentially flat over 1995–2025 (approximately −0.04°C per decade), unlike every other city in this series. However, its monsoon season specifically shows real warming (+0.20°C per decade), suggesting Kathmandu isn't immune to regional climate shifts — the effect is concentrated in one season rather than showing up as a uniform annual trend.

Which seasons are cooling in Kathmandu?

Winter (December–February) and Pre-Monsoon/Hot (March–May) both show genuine cooling trends, at approximately −0.13°C per decade each — nearly identical rates, and both statistically real rather than noise around zero.

Why might Kathmandu be cooling while its neighbors warm?

Several factors are plausible, though establishing the exact cause requires research beyond what trend charts alone can show: Kathmandu's bowl-shaped valley topography is prone to cold-air pooling and temperature inversions, especially in winter and dry pre-monsoon months; and reanalysis data at roughly 9–25km resolution can struggle to fully capture valley-specific microclimate effects that matter more in complex mountain terrain than in flat lowland cities.

How does Kathmandu compare to Rasuwagadhi, another Nepal location in this series?

Dramatically differently, despite being in the same country. Rasuwagadhi, roughly 1,300m higher in elevation, shows the fastest annual warming rate in this entire series (+0.90°C/decade) and the fastest winter warming of any location (+1.67°C/decade) — while Kathmandu's winter is actually cooling. Elevation and topography appear to matter more than country or even regional proximity.

Does Kathmandu's cooling trend mean water security is at risk?

Potentially, yes — and for a different reason than warming would suggest. Winter cooling in Kathmandu is paired with a real decline in winter rainfall (~−30mm/decade), and winter precipitation and snowpack are important for regional river flow later in the dry season. A colder, drier winter trend is worth monitoring for water availability implications, independent of the region's broader warming story.
