Mumbai Climate Trends (1995–2025): Rising Heat, Intensifying Monsoons, and a City on the Coastal Frontline

A 30-year data analysis of temperature and rainfall patterns in Mumbai, Maharashtra, based on ERA5/ERA5-Land reanalysis data — part of our ongoing look at the climate of South Asia.

📍 Mumbai, Maharashtra, India 📅 Data period: 1995–2025 📊 Source: ERA5-Land via Open-Meteo Historical Weather API

Overview: A City Getting Hotter and Wetter at the Same Time

Mumbai, India's largest coastal metropolis and financial capital, sits at the meeting point of the Arabian Sea and one of South Asia's most intense monsoon systems. Three decades of ERA5-Land reanalysis data show a city where both halves of the climate equation — heat and rainfall — are moving in the same direction: up.

The annual mean temperature has climbed from around 26.5°C in the mid-1990s to a record 27.3°C in 2025, while annual precipitation has nearly doubled in range, from roughly 1,300 mm in 1995 to totals now regularly exceeding 2,300–2,700 mm. What makes Mumbai's record distinctive within the region is not just the direction of these trends, but their unevenness — a sharp, recent acceleration in winter and pre-monsoon heat, layered on top of an already-erratic, intensifying monsoon.

The long-term trend line understates what's happened in just the last three years. Mumbai's annual mean temperature jumped from about 26.4°C in 2022 to 27.1°C in 2024 and 27.3°C in 2025 — nearly a full degree of warming in a span that would normally take three to four decades at the underlying trend rate. Whether this marks a genuine step-change or a short-term run of anomalously warm years is a question only the next several years of data will answer, but it is consistent with a broader pattern of accelerating warmth seen across South Asian cities in this dataset.

Season-by-Season Breakdown

Mumbai's climate is shaped by four broad seasons: winter (Dec–Feb), the pre-monsoon hot season (Mar–May), the monsoon (Jun–Sep), and the post-monsoon (Oct–Nov). Each is behaving differently.

Winter (December–February): The Season Warming Fastest, Right Now

Mumbai's "winter" rarely feels cold — mean temperatures have historically sat between 23.5°C and 25°C — but this is also the season showing the sharpest recent acceleration. After hovering in a fairly narrow 24–25°C band for two decades, winter mean temperature jumped from about 23.9°C in 2022 to 25.2°C in 2024 and 26.1°C in 2025, a rise of roughly 2.2°C in three years. Winter precipitation remains the region's driest signal overall — mostly near zero — punctuated by isolated anomalous events, such as 1996 (~103 mm) and 2021 (~104 mm), likely tied to unusual western disturbance or cyclonic activity reaching the coast.

Pre-Monsoon / Hot Season (March–May): The Steadiest Long-Term Warming Trend

This is where Mumbai's warming signal is most consistent and least noisy. Mean temperatures have climbed from around 27.8°C in the mid-1990s to a record 29.3°C in 2025 — a sustained rise of roughly +0.5°C per decade, the fastest steady seasonal trend in the dataset. Precipitation in this normally dry season has also become more erratic, with an unusually large spike in 2021 (~270 mm) and again in 2025 (~185 mm), likely linked to pre-monsoon thunderstorm clusters or cyclonic disturbances in the Arabian Sea.

Monsoon (June–September): More Rain, More Extremes, Muted Heat

The monsoon delivers the overwhelming majority of Mumbai's annual rainfall, and the ERA5 record shows this season intensifying. Total precipitation has risen from roughly 1,500 mm in the mid-1990s to a range now regularly between 2,200 mm and 2,600 mm, with a record 2020 season (~2,620 mm). Mean temperature during the monsoon has moved only slightly, staying close to 26.7–27.0°C — cloud cover and persistent rain appear to be buffering monsoon-season heat even as rainfall totals climb, a pattern distinct from the sharper warming seen in the dry seasons.

Why this matters for Mumbai: a wetter monsoon layered on a city built largely at or near sea level, with limited stormwater capacity, means rising rainfall totals translate directly into higher flood risk — not just more days of rain, but more concentrated, high-intensity rain events of the kind that have repeatedly overwhelmed Mumbai's drainage system in recent monsoons.

Post-Monsoon (October–November): A Quiet but Real Upward Drift

The retreating monsoon season shows a modest but visible upward trend in rainfall, from roughly 50 mm in 1995 to totals commonly above 100–150 mm in the last decade, including an extreme outlier in 2019 (~290 mm) — likely associated with a tropical cyclone or deep depression in the Arabian Sea. Mean temperature in this transition season has stayed comparatively stable around 27–27.5°C, though with some volatility, including a spike to 28.5°C in 2014–15 and a cooler dip to 26°C in 2022.

Extreme Heat: A New Signal in the Record

Tracking days that cross a defined high-temperature extreme-heat threshold, the 30-year ERA5 record for Mumbai stays essentially flat at zero for almost the entire period — and then registers its only flagged occurrence around 2023–24. A single data point doesn't establish a trend on its own, but its timing lines up with the same 2022–2025 window in which Mumbai's annual, winter, and pre-monsoon means all jumped sharply, reinforcing the picture of a compressed, recent acceleration rather than a smooth 30-year climb.

The contrast is instructive: Islamabad's warming is led by its winter season, while Mumbai's steadiest warming shows up in the pre-monsoon hot season, with winter only recently — and abruptly — catching up. Both cities, however, share a pattern seen across the region: whichever season is warming fastest, the acceleration has been most visible in the last three to five years of the record, not spread evenly across three decades.

On rainfall, Mumbai's trajectory is distinct from inland cities like Delhi or Lahore because of its direct coastal exposure — Arabian Sea moisture and cyclonic systems can dump large amounts of rain outside the core monsoon window (as seen in the 2019 post-monsoon and 2021 pre-monsoon spikes), a vulnerability shared more closely with Dhaka and Colombo than with the drier inland cities in this series.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Mumbai's climate getting warmer?

Yes. ERA5-Land reanalysis data from 1995–2025 shows Mumbai's annual mean temperature rising from around 26.5°C to a record 27.3°C in 2025, an overall pace of roughly +0.25°C per decade — with a notably sharper rise in the last three years.

Which season is warming fastest in Mumbai?

Over the full 30-year record, the pre-monsoon/hot season (March–May) shows the steadiest long-term warming, at roughly +0.5°C per decade, rising from about 27.8°C to 29.3°C. However, winter (Dec–Feb) has warmed most sharply in just the last three years, jumping by more than 2°C between 2022 and 2025.

Is Mumbai getting more rainfall?

Yes. Annual total precipitation has risen from around 1,300 mm in the mid-1990s to totals regularly above 2,300–2,700 mm in recent years, driven mainly by an intensifying monsoon season (June–September), which supplies roughly 85% of the city's annual rain.

What data source is used for this analysis?

All figures are derived from ERA5 and ERA5-Land reanalysis data, accessed via the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API, covering January 1995 through 2025. This is the same methodology used across all city profiles on South Asia Climate Trends.

How does Mumbai compare to other South Asian cities?

Mumbai's coastal, monsoon-dominated profile sets it apart from inland cities like Delhi, Lahore, and Islamabad, which show sharper winter warming and drier annual profiles. Mumbai's own warming is led by the pre-monsoon hot season, while its precipitation trend is the most strongly upward of the region's coastal cities alongside Dhaka and Colombo. See the comparison table above for details, and visit each linked city article for full seasonal breakdowns.

Why does Mumbai's monsoon matter so much for the city's climate risk?

Because Mumbai sits largely at or near sea level with dense, aging drainage infrastructure, an intensifying monsoon (rising from ~1,500 mm to over 2,600 mm in peak years) translates directly into higher urban flood risk — not simply more rainy days, but more concentrated high-intensity rain events of the kind that have repeatedly disrupted the city in recent years.
