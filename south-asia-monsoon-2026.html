Is South Asia's 2026 Monsoon Underperforming? What 30 Years of Data Says

Published September 1, 2026 · Regional
Quick answer: Yes, largely as forecast. India's monsoon rainfall for June–August 2026 came in roughly 13.8–14% below the long-period average, and the country just recorded its warmest August since records began in 1901. Forecasters flagged both outcomes months in advance, tied to a developing El Niño. Whether this counts as a real departure from this site's own 30-year trend data depends heavily on which city you're asking about — and for some of them, a weak monsoon year is actually closer to their historical norm than the wetter recent years have been.

Every city report on this site is built from ERA5 reanalysis data running through 2025 — last year's numbers, not this year's. So when a below-normal monsoon gets forecast and then confirmed in near-real time, it's worth pausing to compare the live season against the multi-decade baseline this site tracks. This report does that: what forecasters said back in April, what's actually happened through the end of August, and how that stacks up against the 30-year trends already published for Mumbai, Karachi, Colombo, and Dhaka.

What Forecasters Said, Back in April

On April 30, 2026, the World Meteorological Organization and the South Asian Climate Outlook Forum — representing meteorological services from nine countries — issued their June–September seasonal outlook: below-normal rainfall across much of South Asia, with the strongest signal over central regions, alongside above-normal daytime and nighttime temperatures. The reasoning centered on a developing El Niño, historically associated with suppressed South Asian monsoon rainfall, combined with the Indian Ocean Dipole shifting from neutral toward a positive phase as the season progressed.

India's own Meteorological Department (IMD) put a number on it in its first-stage forecast: monsoon rainfall likely at 92% of the long-period average, squarely in IMD's "below normal" category (90–95% of LPA). Pakistan's Meteorological Department (PMD) issued a similar outlook for its June–September season: a general tendency toward normal-to-below-normal rainfall nationally, with El Niño conditions expected to strengthen through the season — though PMD also flagged that Gilgit-Baltistan, Kashmir, and upper Khyber Pakhtunkhwa could see normal-to-above-normal totals, and that a later-season shift toward positive IOD could partially offset the drying effect in parts of Pakistan.

What Actually Happened, Through August

By the numbers available as this report was published, the forecast has largely verified.

Key Figure: India's June–August 2026 Rainfall Deficit

Approximately 13.8–14% below normal

Independent reporting converges on this figure: a roughly 35% deficit in June, a break-even July, and a 16% deficit in August, compounding to a June–August seasonal shortfall of about 14%. More than half of India's meteorological sub-divisions were reporting rain stress by the end of August. The regional pattern was uneven — Southern Peninsular India (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana) recorded a steeper 24.2% deficiency, and East and North-East India ran 25.7% short, while parts of northern India fared closer to normal.

Key Figure: August 2026 Temperature

India's warmest August since 1901

India's average temperature for the month reached 28.01°C, with the highest average minimum temperature for August on record (24.36°C against a normal of 23.68°C). This is the second half of the same signal forecasters described in April: El Niño years tend to combine below-normal rainfall with above-normal heat, and August 2026 delivered both simultaneously.

Pakistan's season told a more mixed story. PMD's own outlook anticipated a normal-to-below-normal tendency nationally, and the country's National Disaster Management Authority continued issuing daily monsoon situation reports through August — a reminder that "below normal" as a seasonal total doesn't eliminate flash-flood risk from short, intense rainfall spells, which remained an active concern through the peak monsoon weeks even in a below-average year overall.

The outlook for the remainder of the season stayed weak: IMD's September forecast called for continued below-normal rainfall (under 91% of the LPA), and independent forecasters flagged only a brief window for possible partial recovery in the first ten days of September, with a "substantial nationwide recovery" considered unlikely by the season's end.

How This Compares to 30 Years of City-Level Data

Here's the part that matters for interpreting this correctly: a below-normal, El Niño-driven 2026 monsoon does not contradict the rising multi-decade rainfall trends already documented on this site. Interannual variability driven by ENSO and the IOD is layered on top of longer trends, not incompatible with them — a 30-year upward trend line is, almost by definition, built from a mix of unusually wet and unusually dry individual years. 2026 looks likely to be one of the dry ones.

Mumbai — the most exposed city in this series

Mumbai's monsoon supplies roughly 85% of its annual rainfall and has been trending up faster than any other city studied here (~+300mm/decade annually, with the monsoon season itself contributing the bulk of that at roughly +233mm/decade). A weak 2026 monsoon would represent one of the larger single-year downside swings relative to Mumbai's recent, wetter baseline — worth watching closely once full-season 2026 data becomes available.

Karachi — a below-normal year may look closer to "typical"

As our Karachi rainfall deep-dive found, Karachi's rising 30-year trend (+88.7mm/decade) is heavily skewed by a handful of extreme years — above all 2022, at roughly 830mm. Most individual years in Karachi's record sit well under 250mm. A below-normal, El Niño-suppressed 2026 monsoon is arguably more consistent with Karachi's typical year than with the outlier-driven average the trend line implies.

Dhaka — a weak monsoon fits an already-declining trend

Dhaka is the one city in this series with a clearly declining 30-year annual rainfall trend (~−140mm/decade). A below-normal 2026 monsoon wouldn't be a departure from Dhaka's own trajectory so much as another data point consistent with it.

Colombo — less exposed to this particular signal

Colombo's monsoon (June–September) is actually its flattest-trending season (~+17mm/decade) even though Colombo's overall annual rainfall trend is one of the steepest in this series (~+170mm/decade) — driven more by its pre-monsoon and post-monsoon months. Colombo also receives a second, partly independent monsoon later in the year, so a weak southwest monsoon this season may show up less clearly in Colombo's numbers than in India's or Pakistan's.

A methodology note: everything above about the 2026 season itself comes from official real-time reporting (IMD, PMD, WMO, and independent meteorological services), not from this site's own ERA5 reanalysis — 2026 isn't in our charted trend lines yet, which run through 2025. Once the season closes and full-year reanalysis data becomes available, we'll be able to place 2026 precisely on each city's own trend chart rather than relying on national-level seasonal statistics as a proxy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is South Asia's 2026 monsoon below normal?

Yes, largely as forecast. India's June–August 2026 monsoon rainfall came in roughly 13.8–14% below the long-period average, with over half of India's meteorological sub-divisions facing rain stress. Pakistan's monsoon outlook was also normal-to-below-normal for most of the country.

Why is the 2026 monsoon weaker than normal?

Forecasters point primarily to a developing El Niño, which is historically associated with suppressed South Asian monsoon rainfall. The Indian Ocean Dipole was expected to shift toward a positive phase during the season, a factor that can partially offset El Niño's drying effect in some areas but not fully counteract it.

Does a below-normal 2026 monsoon contradict the rising rainfall trends this site has documented?

No. A single El Niño-driven deficit year is short-term interannual variability layered on top of a multi-decade trend, not a contradiction of it. Cities like Mumbai and Colombo have shown rising 30-year rainfall trends specifically because they include both unusually wet and unusually dry years — 2026 is on track to be one of the dry ones.

Which South Asian city is most exposed to a weak monsoon?

Mumbai, where the monsoon supplies roughly 85% of annual rainfall and has been trending up the fastest of any city in this series (~+300mm/decade annually). A weak monsoon year there would represent one of the largest single-year swings relative to its recent baseline.

How does India's warmest August since 1901 relate to the monsoon deficit?

They're two sides of the same climate signal forecasters flagged months in advance: El Niño conditions were expected to bring both below-normal rainfall and above-normal temperatures to the region simultaneously, and August 2026 delivered exactly that combination.
