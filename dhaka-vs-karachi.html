Dhaka vs. Karachi: Warming Fast vs. Barely at All

Published September 9, 2026 · Regional Comparison
Quick answer: Dhaka is warming more than twice as fast as Karachi (+0.30°C/decade vs. +0.13°C/decade) — but the rainfall picture flips entirely. Karachi's precipitation is surging (+88.7mm/decade), while Dhaka's is declining (~−140mm/decade). Two major South Asian coastal-adjacent cities, moving in almost opposite directions on both metrics.

Dhaka and Karachi are two of South Asia's largest, most consequential coastal-adjacent cities — Bangladesh's low-lying delta capital and Pakistan's Arabian Sea port megacity. Using the same 30-year ERA5/ERA5-Land reanalysis methodology applied throughout this site, their climate trajectories turn out to be a genuine study in contrasts, not a case of "two similar cities telling the same story." This report puts them side by side directly, drawing on our full Dhaka climate trend report and Karachi climate trend report.

The Temperature Story: Steady Climb vs. Slow Grind

Dhaka's annual mean temperature shows the clearest, steadiest warming trajectory of any city in this entire series — climbing fairly consistently from around 25.0–25.3°C in the mid-1990s to a record spike near 26.5°C in 2025, the highest point in its 30-year record. Karachi's temperature trend is real but far weaker — from roughly 25.6–26.8°C, with significant year-to-year volatility rather than a smooth climb, driven by natural variability (ENSO cycles, regional atmospheric patterns) layered on top of a modest underlying trend.

Karachi's warming rate is "broadly consistent with the global average land-surface warming trend," according to our full Karachi report — suggesting its warming reflects a wider regional and global pattern rather than a strong local driver. Dhaka's rate, more than double that, points to something adding on top of the regional signal — likely its inland delta exposure without Karachi's coastal moderation.

The Rainfall Paradox

This is where the comparison gets genuinely counterintuitive. Bangladesh is globally known for flood risk; Pakistan's Sindh coast is popularly associated with drought and water scarcity. The 30-year data says something closer to the opposite about each city's trend (not their absolute totals — Dhaka's baseline rainfall remains far higher than Karachi's in any given year).

  • Karachi's rainfall is intensifying — but almost entirely through rare, extreme monsoon years rather than a steady climb. 2022 alone (~830mm) is by far the highest annual total in Karachi's 30-year record, nearly four times typical totals from a decade earlier. This is "rarer but far more intense rainfall events," consistent with what a warming, moisture-holding atmosphere is expected to produce.
  • Dhaka's rainfall is declining — from a trend-line baseline near 2,100–2,200mm in the mid-1990s down to roughly 1,700–1,750mm by 2025. The decline is concentrated almost entirely in the monsoon season itself (~−117mm/decade), which is also, confusingly, Dhaka's fastest-warming season (+0.40°C/decade) — a warming monsoon delivering less rain over time.
An important caveat: a declining long-term rainfall trend doesn't mean declining flood risk for Dhaka. Bangladesh's flood exposure is driven as much by river systems, storm surge, and land subsidence as by local rainfall totals — this data speaks only to Dhaka's own precipitation trend, not the country's broader flood risk profile. Karachi's rising trend, meanwhile, is concentrated enough in single extreme events (2022, and the 2020 monsoon before it) that it has directly overwhelmed urban drainage infrastructure not designed for such volumes.

Extreme Heat: Near-Zero vs. Clustered Spikes

Neither city sees much extreme heat by the standards of this series (compare Delhi or Lahore, which regularly cross 20+ days a year) — but they get there differently.

CityExtreme Heat Pattern
DhakaEssentially zero days/year (≥40°C) across the entire 30-year record — a single exception, ~3 days around 2023
KarachiClustered spikes — most years 0–1 days, but occasional years hit 3–5 days (notably 2007–08, 2015, 2018), rather than a smooth trend

Karachi's own report describes this as a public-health-relevant distinction: "it's experiencing more frequent and more intense heatwave clusters, punctuated by comparatively milder years" — meaning heat-early-warning systems need to plan for sudden severe spikes, not a gradual creeping baseline. Dhaka's near-total absence of extreme heat, meanwhile, reflects its humid, delta climate — very different from Karachi's coastal-desert exposure.

Both cities warm fastest during the monsoon — but that's where the similarity ends. Karachi's monsoon pairs its fastest warming with its sharpest rainfall increase; Dhaka's pairs its fastest warming with its sharpest rainfall decrease. Same season, same direction of temperature change, opposite rainfall consequence.

What Explains the Difference?

  • Geography and exposure. Karachi's direct Arabian Sea coastal position moderates its temperature swings even as warming ocean waters fuel more energetic, extreme monsoon rainfall events. Dhaka's inland delta position, without that coastal buffering, appears to warm faster while its monsoon rainfall trend moves independently — possibly reflecting shifting regional monsoon circulation patterns that don't affect the two cities symmetrically.
  • Trend character, not just direction. Karachi's rainfall increase is a small-number-of-extreme-years story (2022 dominates); Dhaka's decline is a broader, more consistent multi-decade slide. Both are real trends, but they'd call for very different water-infrastructure planning — Karachi needs drainage built for rare severe events, Dhaka needs planning for a potentially lower sustained baseline alongside continued flood risk from non-rainfall sources.
  • Recent-year acceleration in Dhaka. Several of Dhaka's charts hit their single highest point in the entire 30-year record within the final 1–3 years of data — too recent to call a confirmed new trend, but worth monitoring as more data accumulates.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Dhaka warming faster than Karachi?

Yes, by more than double. Dhaka's annual warming rate is approximately +0.30°C per decade, compared to Karachi's +0.13°C per decade — making Dhaka one of the fastest-warming lowland cities in this series and Karachi one of the slowest.

Which city has more extreme heat, Dhaka or Karachi?

Neither sees much, but in different ways. Dhaka records essentially zero days per year at or above 40°C across the entire 30-year record, with a single exception around 2023. Karachi sees occasional clustered spikes of 3–5 days in certain years, driven by its coastal-desert exposure, but remains low overall.

Is Dhaka or Karachi getting more rain?

Karachi's rainfall is rising sharply (+88.7mm per decade), driven by a small number of extreme monsoon years, especially 2022. Dhaka's rainfall is declining (approximately −140mm per decade) over the same period — the opposite direction, despite Bangladesh's reputation for flood risk.

Why is Dhaka warming faster than Karachi despite getting less rain?

The two cities have very different geography. Dhaka is an inland delta city without Karachi's coastal moderation, and its monsoon season is both warming fastest of any season (+0.40°C/decade) and losing rainfall (−117mm/decade) — a warming, drying monsoon. Karachi's coastal position and Arabian Sea proximity keep its temperature trend modest even as monsoon rainfall intensifies dramatically.

Does declining rainfall mean Dhaka has less flood risk than Karachi?

Not necessarily. Dhaka's flood risk is driven as much by river systems, storm surge, and land subsidence as by local rainfall totals, so a declining precipitation trend doesn't straightforwardly translate to declining flood risk. Karachi's rising rainfall trend, by contrast, is concentrated in a small number of extreme events that have directly overwhelmed urban drainage infrastructure.
